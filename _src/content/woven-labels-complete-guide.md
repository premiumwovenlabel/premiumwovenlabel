## What Are Woven Labels?

A woven label is a narrow piece of fabric made by interlacing yarns on a label loom. Its lettering, logo or pattern is built from coloured yarns during weaving. It is not simply ink printed on top of a finished fabric.

Woven labels are used on clothing, accessories, bags, footwear, uniforms, home textiles, toys and handmade products. A label can identify a brand, size, collection, origin, material composition or care instructions. A single label can carry more than one kind of information, provided the content remains legible and meets the rules for the market where the finished product is sold.

The best woven label is selected for its job. A detailed neck logo, a soft-feeling care label and a low-cost size tab may each need a different weave, fold and finish.

> **Important:** A woven brand label and a legally required garment-content or care label are not automatically the same thing. The finished product’s manufacturer, importer or seller is responsible for checking the rules in each destination market.

## How Woven Labels Are Constructed

### Warp and weft

Woven fabric is formed from two yarn systems:

- **Warp yarns** run lengthwise through the label and are held under tension on the loom.
- **Weft yarns** pass across the width and interlace with the warp.

The pattern is created by controlling which yarns pass over or under others. Different interlacings change the face, texture, density, shine and level of detail. On a woven label, yarn selection and loom settings affect the label’s look and feel as much as the artwork does.

### Jacquard is a weaving method

“Jacquard” describes a pattern-control method that allows the loom to weave complex designs. It is not simply another name for damask, satin or taffeta. A supplier may use a Jacquard loom to produce a particular label weave. Ask the supplier to identify the actual weave, resolution and finish being quoted.

### Woven versus printed labels

| Woven label | Printed label |
|---|---|
| Design is made from yarns woven into the fabric | Design is applied to a substrate using ink, heat transfer or another print process |
| Has a textile surface and woven texture | Can reproduce fine text, gradients or photographic detail more easily, depending on print method |
| Usually chosen for visible branding and a fabric-like finish | Often chosen for dense care instructions, small text or smooth tagless applications |
| Can be folded and finished in many ways | Can be cut, folded, coated or applied as a heat transfer, depending on construction |
| Colourfastness and legibility depend on yarn, dye, construction, use and testing | Performance depends on the printing system, ink, substrate, curing and testing |

A woven design may be less suitable than printing when the artwork contains gradients, tiny legal text or photographic detail. Conversely, a printed design may not deliver the textured, thread-built look a brand wants. Neither method is automatically “better”; select for the application and verify the sample.

## Main Types of Woven Labels

Suppliers use slightly different names and quality grades. Request a physical sample or a clear specification before approving production.

### 1. Damask

Damask is a dense, detailed woven-label option. It is often selected for logos with fine lettering, narrow lines or several visual details. Standard and high-definition versions may be offered. “High definition” is a supplier quality description, not a universal performance grade, so compare actual samples.

**Often used for:** premium neck labels, brand tabs, fashion labels and detailed logos.

**Selection notes:** A fine design can still lose definition if the finished label is too small, contrast is low or text is too thin. Ask the mill to review the original artwork at the final dimensions.

### 2. Satin

Satin constructions have a smoother face and a soft sheen. They can make simple logos and wordmarks look polished, and are often chosen where a soft hand is important.

**Often used for:** neck labels, lingerie, soft garments, accessories and labels where a subtle shine suits the brand.

**Selection notes:** Fine detail may not reproduce as clearly as on a dense damask construction. Ask whether the quoted satin is single-face, reverse satin or another construction, and inspect both sides.

### 3. Taffeta

Taffeta is generally a flatter, firmer and economical option for simpler artwork. It suits strong lettering and straightforward graphics, especially where a soft premium hand or very fine detail is not the priority.

**Often used for:** size tabs, care or content panels, uniforms, volume products and simpler brand labels.

**Selection notes:** A bold design usually works better than tiny type or delicate lines. A lower unit price may not mean a lower total order cost once setup, finishing, freight and waste are included.

### 4. Double-sided, tubular and specialty constructions

Some mills offer labels woven with different faces, two layers or a tubular construction. These can show artwork on both sides, create a thicker hand or hide the reverse in a fold. Availability, minimum quantity and finishing are supplier-specific.

### 5. Textured and other weave effects

Twill-like ribs, reverse satin, high-density surfaces, raised effects and metallic yarn accents may be available. These are finishing or construction choices rather than a single universal label category. They can change readability, skin feel, cost and care performance.

## Materials and Yarns

The yarn affects appearance, hand, durability, care performance and sustainability claims. Confirm the label’s composition if the content is relevant to your customers or to a claim.

### Polyester

Polyester is widely used for woven labels because it is available in many colours and constructions and is suitable for many apparel applications. Its exact performance still depends on yarn quality, dye, construction, finish and the garment’s care conditions.

### Recycled polyester

Some suppliers offer yarn made from recycled polyester. Ask for evidence that applies to the actual yarn and finished label. If making a certified recycled-content claim, check the applicable certification, product scope, supply-chain documents and label-use rules. “Recycled” should not be used as a general environmental promise without support.

### Cotton and other natural-fibre yarns

Cotton or other natural-fibre yarns may be available for a particular appearance or product brief. They can behave differently from polyester in washing, shrinkage, abrasion, colour and softness. Confirm performance on the assembled garment rather than assuming the label will behave like the garment fabric.

### Nylon, specialty and metallic yarns

Some suppliers offer nylon or specialty yarns, including metallic-look yarns. These can change sheen, strength, stiffness, comfort and care requirements. Ask for samples and confirm whether the yarn is suitable for direct skin contact and the product’s laundering or finishing process.

### Colour matching

Thread colours are usually selected from a supplier’s yarn chart or a custom-dyed palette. A Pantone or other colour reference is a target; it does not guarantee a perfect match because the actual yarn, dye lot, weave and lighting affect appearance. For close brand matching, ask for a dyed yarn or woven strike-off, agree a colour tolerance and approve the physical sample under consistent lighting.

## Edge Finishes, Cuts and Folds

The edge finish affects comfort, durability, appearance and how the label is attached. Names differ between manufacturers, so a diagram with fold lines is often clearer than a fold name alone.

### Common edge finishes

- **Straight or heat cut:** The label is cut to size and the edge is sealed by heat. It is often used for flat labels, but the edge can feel firmer than a folded edge.
- **Laser cut:** A laser cuts the label to a shape or size. The result depends on material and equipment; ask whether the edge may be stiff or darkened.
- **Ultrasonic or other sealed cut:** Some suppliers use other cutting and edge-sealing methods.
- **Woven selvedge / woven edge:** The edge is formed during weaving rather than cut. It can provide a clean edge but may limit the shape or dimensions.
- **Slit edge:** A continuous woven ribbon is divided into individual labels and the sides are cut or sealed.

### Common fold styles

| Fold | Typical construction and use |
|---|---|
| Straight cut / flat | No fold. Sew around the perimeter or catch an edge into a seam. |
| Centre fold | Folded in half so the two ends are caught into a seam; the artwork sits on the outer face. |
| End fold | Left and right ends are turned under; often used as a neck label sewn along the top and bottom edges. |
| Loop fold | The label is folded so it can be caught into a seam while a loop or flag portion remains visible. Often used at a side seam or hem. |
| Mitre fold | Ends are folded back, commonly to make a tab or loop caught into a seam. |
| Manhattan / book-style fold | A supplier-specific fold that may combine a turned end and a centre fold. Ask for the factory’s diagram because naming is not consistent. |

Before ordering, confirm whether the size in the quote refers to the **finished visible size** or the **unfolded loom size**. Also confirm which edges are sewn, which edges remain exposed and how much seam allowance is needed.

## Choosing the Right Label for the Product

| Product or placement | Common starting point | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Neck brand label | Damask or satin; often end fold or flat sew-in | Skin comfort, text size, seam width and how the back feels |
| Size tab | Taffeta or damask; often a small centre-fold or end tab | Readability at small size and consistency across the size range |
| Care/content label | Often a printed label or a woven panel with sufficient space | Legal wording, care-symbol requirements, language, permanence and legibility |
| Side-seam or hem flag | Damask, satin or taffeta; loop or centre fold | Visible portion, fold direction and seam allowance |
| Bag, cap or outerwear branding | Damask, taffeta, specialty weave or patch-style flat label | Exposure to abrasion, moisture, sun, dirt and cleaning |
| Premium gift, handmade or home-textile product | Select by brand feel and application | Attachment method, washing conditions and whether edges will fray or irritate |

There is no universal “standard size” for all woven labels. Choose a finished size that fits the garment, leaves room for sewing and keeps every character legible. A supplier can recommend loom-specific limits, but the sample sewn into the actual product is the final check.

## Preparing Artwork for a Woven Label

A strong woven-label design is simple enough to survive conversion from a screen graphic to a small textile structure.

### Artwork checklist

1. **Send vector artwork where possible.** PDF, AI, EPS or SVG files are useful if the supplier can open them. If only raster artwork is available, send a high-resolution file and identify its intended finished size.
2. **State the finished dimensions.** Give width and height in millimetres or inches and say whether this is the visible folded size or the flat, unfolded size.
3. **Identify the front and back.** Mark the reading direction, face side, fold lines and any information intended for the reverse.
4. **Use clear, high-contrast shapes.** Small lettering and thin strokes need more space and stronger contrast than large bold graphics.
5. **Avoid assuming a gradient will weave smoothly.** A gradient may need to be simplified into solid colour blocks or a limited number of thread colours.
6. **Provide colour targets.** List brand references and ask the supplier to confirm the closest available yarn colours. Approve a physical sample for critical colour matching.
7. **Convert fonts to outlines when appropriate.** This prevents missing-font substitutions. Keep a separate editable master for future changes.
8. **Allow space for sewing and finishing.** Keep important artwork away from cut edges and fold lines.
9. **Ask for a production proof.** Review spelling, sizing, alignment, colour, reverse side and folding. Approve a physical sample when comfort or colour is important.

There is no single minimum font size or line width that works across every factory and weave. Ask the supplier to state the smallest text and finest line it can produce reliably at your chosen dimensions, then approve a woven sample rather than relying only on a digital mock-up.

## How Woven Labels Are Made

The exact factory process varies, but a typical order moves through these steps:

1. **Brief and specification:** The buyer provides artwork, size, material preference, quantity, placement, fold, colour references and delivery destination.
2. **Artwork preparation:** The supplier converts the visual design into a loom-readable pattern and checks whether the lettering and detail can be woven at the selected size.
3. **Yarn and colour preparation:** The supplier selects stock yarn colours or arranges dyed yarn for a closer match.
4. **Warp preparation:** Lengthwise yarns are arranged and placed under tension for the loom.
5. **Weaving:** The loom interlaces warp and weft yarns to build the design into continuous narrow fabric.
6. **Inspection:** The supplier checks weaving, colour, pattern, width and visible defects according to its process.
7. **Cutting and finishing:** The continuous woven tape is cut, sealed, shaped, folded or otherwise finished to the agreed construction.
8. **Final check and packing:** The order is counted, inspected, bundled or packed, and prepared for dispatch.

Approve the digital proof and sample before bulk production. A change to artwork, thread colour, size, fold or material after approval may require a new setup or sample and can affect cost and timing.

## Advantages and Limitations

### Advantages

- The design is integrated into the textile rather than sitting only as a printed surface.
- Woven construction gives the label texture and a tactile brand detail.
- Labels can be made with many combinations of weave, fold, size and edge finish.
- They can combine a logo, size, collection name or short message.
- A well-specified and tested label can be suitable for repeated garment care.

### Limitations

- Tiny text and fine lines may not reproduce clearly.
- Gradients and photographic art usually need simplification.
- Labels can have a firmer edge or feel uncomfortable if the material, cut or placement is poorly chosen.
- Yarn colours may not exactly match a screen or print standard without a physical approval process.
- The most suitable weave and finish depend on the supplier’s equipment and order quantity.
- A woven label does not replace required garment disclosures simply because it is woven.

## Quality Control and Testing

A label can look correct on a screen and still fail after sewing or laundering. Set the quality requirements before placing the order.

### Inspect the physical sample for

- Correct spelling, logo proportions, colour and reading direction
- Finished and unfolded dimensions, including fold and seam allowance
- Legibility at actual viewing distance
- Edge softness, stiffness and potential irritation
- Consistency of colour across the face and reverse
- Secure weaving, clean cuts, no loose or broken yarns, and no excessive fraying
- Fold position and compatibility with the garment seam
- Appearance and attachment after the product’s actual washing, drying or cleaning process

### Agree a written specification

Record the label construction, yarn/material, dimensions and tolerance, colours, artwork revision, fold diagram, edge finish, quantity, approved sample reference, packaging and any required testing. State who approves substitutions and how defects or short shipments will be handled.

### Useful textile test references

Testing is not automatically required for every woven label, but a brand may set test requirements based on use, customer standards and destination markets.

- **ISO 6330:2021** describes domestic washing and drying procedures used for textile testing. Each procedure represents a wash; a complete test combines a washing and drying procedure.
- **ISO 105-C06:2010** describes colour-fastness tests for domestic and commercial laundering.
- **ISO 105-X12:2016** describes testing colour resistance to rubbing, including dry and wet rubbing.

These methods assess defined properties under defined conditions; they do not guarantee performance in every real-world laundry situation. The garment brand should test the label sewn into the finished garment, using the care conditions it plans to recommend. See [ISO 6330](https://www.iso.org/standard/75934.html), [ISO 105-C06](https://committee.iso.org/standard/51276.html) and [ISO 105-X12](https://www.iso.org/standard/65207.html).

## Sustainability and Product-Safety Claims

A label can contribute to a product’s material story, but specific environmental or safety statements need evidence.

### Recycled-content claims

Recycled polyester may be available for label yarn. If a brand wants to make a certified claim, confirm that the supplier’s certification covers the facility, material, product and transaction. Textile Exchange explains that product-related claims under its standards require an unbroken certified chain of custody through the relevant supply chain; a supplier’s general certificate alone may not prove that a particular shipment qualifies. See [Textile Exchange’s standards FAQs](https://hub.textileexchange.org/home/site-wide-content/standards-faqs).

### Harmful-substance testing

OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 is a product-safety label for textiles tested for harmful substances. It is not by itself proof that a label is recycled, organic, biodegradable or made in an environmentally responsible factory. Confirm that the specific article and components are within the current certificate scope. See the [OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 overview](https://www.oeko-tex.com/en/our-standards/oeko-tex-standard-100).

### Make precise claims

Prefer specific, substantiated statements such as “woven with 100% recycled polyester yarn” when the evidence supports that exact statement. Avoid vague terms such as “eco-friendly,” “green” or “sustainable” unless the claim is clearly defined and supported. The UK Competition and Markets Authority’s fashion guidance discusses clear fibre claims and evidence: [environmental claims in fashion retail](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/complying-with-consumer-law-when-making-environmental-claims-in-the-fashion-retail-sector/complying-with-consumer-law-when-making-environmental-claims-in-the-fashion-retail-sector).

## Global Labeling Rules: What Brands Need to Know

Woven-label specifications and garment-label laws are different topics. The rules below concern the **finished textile product** and are only a starting point. Product category, sales channel, language, origin, exceptions and local amendments can change what is required. Check the current official guidance for every country where the product will be sold.

| Market | High-level requirement to review | Practical point |
|---|---|---|
| United States | The FTC’s Textile Fiber Rule requires covered textile products to disclose generic fibre names and percentages, country of origin, and manufacturer/marketer identity. A separate FTC Care Labeling Rule covers care instructions for clothing and certain piece goods. | Do not assume a decorative brand label meets these disclosures. The business name or an eligible FTC RN may identify the responsible business. Care instructions need a reasonable basis. |
| European Union | Textile products in scope generally need fibre composition on a durable, legible, visible and accessible label or marking; products with at least 80% textile fibres are included. Fibre names and percentages are shown in descending order, and required wording must be in the official language(s) of the countries where the product is sold. | Keep the composition visibly separate from other information such as care instructions. Confirm the fibre names against the regulation’s permitted list. |
| Great Britain | UK government guidance says textile products must show fibre content; different-fibre components may need separate composition information. | Check whether the product is being placed on the market in Great Britain or Northern Ireland and confirm the applicable current rules. |
| Canada | Textile labels generally need fibre information and dealer identity. Permanent or non-permanent label rules depend on the product. Fibre disclosure is generally bilingual, subject to regional rules; Quebec has additional French-language requirements. Care labelling is voluntary, but voluntary claims must be accurate. | Check the garment category, English/French wording, label permanence and Quebec requirements before artwork approval. |
| Australia | Care labelling is mandatory for covered clothing and textile products; instructions may use English wording, international care symbols, or both. The ACCC’s scope list excludes a standalone “woven label,” while garments and other listed products may still be covered. | This exclusion is about the woven-label accessory as a product; it does not remove the care-labelling duties that may apply to the garment carrying it. |
| Japan | Japan’s Household Goods Quality Labeling framework applies to designated product categories. Current textile rules specify fibre-composition information and care symbols; the Consumer Affairs Agency references JIS L0001:2024, and care information generally needs a durable attachment. | Do not rely on a foreign care-symbol set or English-only text without checking the Japanese rules and product category. |
| Other markets | National rules may set fibre names, language, origin statements, care symbols, safety warnings or product-specific disclosures. | Check the local regulator and standards in the actual destination market; an international standard does not automatically replace national legal requirements. |

## Care Symbols and Care Instructions

Care labels tell customers and professional cleaners how to care for the finished textile product. The correct instructions should be based on the most delicate component and on the product’s real performance.

The international care-symbol system is associated with ISO 3758 and GINETEX. The usual sequence is washing, bleaching, drying, ironing and professional textile care. GINETEX states that its symbols are protected trademarks and may require a licence; do not copy a symbol set from an unverified image search or assume that every market accepts the same artwork. Check the requirements and symbol rights for your target market. See [GINETEX care-labelling principles](https://www.ginetex.net/gb/labelling/care-labelling/) and [ISO 3758:2023](https://www.iso.org/obp/ui/en/).

If care symbols or words are woven into a small label, make sure they remain distinct at the final size. A symbol that is technically present but unreadable is not useful to the customer and may not satisfy local rules.

## How to Request a Quote

Send a complete brief so suppliers can quote the same specification.

### Quote-request checklist

- Finished product and placement: neck, side seam, hem, bag, cap, home textile or other
- Quantity per design, plus number of designs and size variants
- Artwork file and revision number
- Finished visible width and height, with units
- Unfolded dimensions, if folded
- Weave preference: damask, satin, taffeta or supplier recommendation
- Yarn/material requirements and any certified-material claim
- Thread-colour references and colour tolerance
- Edge finish, cut method and fold diagram
- Front/back artwork and reading direction
- How the label will be sewn or otherwise attached
- Destination countries and any required content or care statements
- Sample requirement, approved test methods and acceptance criteria
- Packaging, delivery address, delivery date, shipping method and any import terms

Ask the supplier to break out setup or sampling charges, unit cost by quantity, minimum order quantity, sample timing, bulk timing, freight, payment terms and any conditions that could change the quote. Prices and lead times vary with the factory, quantity, yarn, weave, colours, finishing, certification, testing and shipping; there is no reliable universal price or delivery promise.

## Common Ordering Mistakes

1. **Quoting a logo without its finished size.** Fine details may fail when reduced.
2. **Approving only a screen proof.** A screen cannot show the final hand, woven resolution or exact yarn colour.
3. **Confusing flat size with folded size.** The factory may quote unfolded dimensions.
4. **Choosing the lowest-cost weave before checking legibility.** A simple label may suit taffeta; dense lettering may need another construction.
5. **Ignoring the exposed edge.** A stiff edge against skin can make a technically correct label unpleasant to wear.
6. **Leaving out fold and sewing information.** The garment factory may attach the label incorrectly or hide the design.
7. **Using care symbols or fibre statements without compliance review.** A label supplier may manufacture the artwork exactly as supplied without validating it for every market.
8. **Making an unsupported recycled or safety claim.** Request evidence that covers the particular material or finished product.
9. **Changing artwork after sample approval.** A late change can affect setup, approval, cost and delivery.
10. **Testing the label alone instead of the garment assembly.** A garment’s fabric, dyes, trims and label interact during care.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are woven labels washable?

They can be suitable for washable garments, but performance depends on the yarn, dye, construction, edge finish, sewing and care conditions. Test the label attached to the finished garment under the intended care process.

### Which woven label type is best?

Damask is a common starting point for detailed designs; satin is selected for a smooth, softly shiny appearance; taffeta is often chosen for simple designs and cost-sensitive applications. Compare samples because suppliers’ constructions differ.

### Is Jacquard the same as damask?

No. Jacquard describes a way to control complex patterns on a loom. Damask describes a label weave or quality family. A damask label can be woven using a Jacquard system.

### Can I put care instructions on a woven label?

Yes, if the label has enough space and the text or symbols remain clear. Check which instructions, symbols, language, permanence and placement are accepted in each destination market.

### Are woven labels itchy?

They can be, depending on yarn, density, edge sealing, fold, garment placement and skin sensitivity. Request a soft-hand sample and sew it into the garment before bulk approval.

### Can a woven label reproduce a gradient?

A gradient is usually converted into stepped colour areas or a simplified design. Ask for an artwork review and woven sample if the gradient is important.

### How many colours can a woven label have?

The maximum depends on the label loom, construction, design and supplier. Ask each factory for its maximum, any extra colour charges and the available thread palette.

### What file format should I send?

Vector artwork such as AI, EPS, SVG or vector PDF is usually easiest to prepare. Include a high-resolution raster version if useful, specify final dimensions and identify all colours. Confirm acceptable formats with the manufacturer.

### How do I verify recycled or certified material?

Request the current certificate, scope, exact product/material listing and any transaction documents needed for the claim. Confirm that the claim and logo use are allowed for the specific product and selling market.

### Do woven labels have a universal minimum order quantity?

No. Minimums vary by factory, loom setup, material, finish and design count. Ask for the minimum for each exact specification and whether multiple sizes or colours can share setup.

## Final Selection Checklist

Before production, confirm all of the following:

- The label type suits the design and product
- The sample is readable, comfortable and correctly coloured
- The finished and unfolded dimensions are clear
- The fold, edge finish and stitch placement are shown in a diagram
- The yarn/material and any certification claims are documented
- The garment’s legal composition, care, language and origin statements have been reviewed for each destination market
- Any care-symbol licence or market-specific symbol requirement has been checked
- The approved sample, artwork revision and written tolerances are recorded
- The sewn-in garment assembly has been checked under the intended care conditions
